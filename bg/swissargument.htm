A Case for Standard Swiss Pairing over Modern FIDE Pairing Systems


FIDE’s pairing algorithms (Dutch, Dubov, Lim, and Burstein) aim to reduce the number of upfloats and downfloats between score brackets as well as the number of double colours (WW and BB). The question is why.

FIDE seems to assume that a colour sequence such as WBWBWBWBW is better than WWBBWWBBW. However, this is not self-evident. On the contrary, one could argue that the latter pattern is advantageous for the player. Someone playing the same colour as in the previous round recalls their preparation more easily and does not need to readjust mentally. After all, playing with White versus Black involves two fundamentally different tasks. A tennis player would hardly want to switch between grass and clay before every match.

One can therefore argue that a player who receives the colour sequence BBWWBBWWB is favoured over a player who receives BWBWBWBWB. If this is true, the colour restrictions used in Dutch, Dubov, and Burstein may paradoxically increase rather than decrease unfairness. In a 9-round tournament with 60 participants, roughly 45% of players receive perfect colour alternation, while the remaining players may get sequences such as WBWWBBWWB or BWBWBBWWB.

How does this increase fairness? If double colours actually represent an advantage, it seems fairer for all players to receive roughly the same type of colour distribution. This is also what happens in normal Swiss pairing, where only triple colours are prevented while both double colours and float history are otherwise ignored. In such a system, practically all players receive double colours. Dutch, Dubov, and Burstein, on the other hand, result in nearly half the players getting perfect colour alternation while the rest get double colours. If double colours are advantageous, this means that some players are deprived of a benefit that other players enjoy.

The likely explanation is that players have complained when given three Black games in a row, which is completely understandable. This happened frequently in the past when pairings were done manually with cards, for example under the Monrad system. There, a player could even get four games in a row with the same colour.

However, it does not follow from this that two games in a row with the same colour is a problem. Because triple colours are undesirable, it has been assumed that double colours should also be avoided. This has led to restrictions that limit pairing freedom without any proof that double colours carry a disadvantage. Triple colours never appear to occur in normal Swiss pairing.

FIDE’s pairing algorithms also suppress floats between score brackets (floaters), albeit not as radically. A downfloat means that a player is paired against an opponent with a lower score. An upfloat means the opposite. In Dutch, Dubov, and Burstein, floats are reduced by approximately 20% compared to normal Swiss pairing.

In this respect too, natural Swiss pairing is degraded. Repeated upfloats are viewed as a potential disadvantage, as the player may face stronger opponents, and repeated downfloats as a potential advantage, as the player may face easier opposition. Historically, multiple upfloats or downfloats for the same player have been regarded negatively because FIDE views floats as an advantage or disadvantage that should be distributed evenly among participants — not because there is strong evidence that repeated floats reduce tournament quality.

In modern Swiss pairing, players within each score bracket are ranked by tiebreak points. The player ranked highest in the bracket has performed best and is therefore the natural candidate for an upfloat. Such a player often faces an opponent in the higher score bracket whose tiebreak score is lower than his own. Correspondingly, the lowest-ranked player is the natural candidate for a downfloat. Floats are therefore not an unfair element, but a natural consequence of the players’ standings in the tournament. A player leading a score bracket has, through his results, earned a tougher test — something many players also welcome. The lowest-ranked player has correspondingly earned an opportunity to recover against slightly weaker opposition.

If one compares the number of floats in a tournament with 60 participants and 9 rounds, the question arises whether the reduction from approximately 83 floats (in normal Swiss pairing) to approximately 70 floats actually leads to any measurable improvement in tournament results. The Dutch system sacrifices pairing freedom to eliminate about 13 floats. What measurable benefit is achieved by eliminating these floats? This mindset may, for instance, have emerged from recurring complaints such as, "I was floated up three times." However, such objections do not prove that repeated floats are inherently unfair, especially towards the end of the tournament when score brackets become smaller.

Conclusion

FIDE’s pairing systems appear largely to optimize formal criteria rather than competitive fairness. By counteracting double colours and floats, pairing freedom is restricted without any demonstration that these restrictions lead to better tournament outcomes.

Normal Swiss pairing contains fewer artificial restrictions and allows the dynamics of the tournament to govern which players meet to a greater extent. Therefore, it appears to be a more natural and likely also a fairer alternative. The fact that the Dubov system can occasionally result in the two tournament leaders never having met by the end of the final round illustrates how far modern algorithms can deviate from the fundamental idea of the Swiss system: that the best-performing players should progressively be pitted against one another.

FIDE should instead support normal Swiss pairing: seeded pairing in the first round, a ban on triple colours, general colour balance (so that players receive roughly as many White as Black games), and ranking within score brackets by points, tiebreak score, and TPN. Double colours and float history, however, should not influence the pairings.
